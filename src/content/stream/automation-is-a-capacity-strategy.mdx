Automation is one of the few durable ways to increase capacity without increasing headcount. Most organisations still treat it like a hobby.

## The trap

In most organisations, automation lives as a side project — a handful of scripts, owned by whoever happened to build them, growing one workflow at a time. It works, until it doesn't. Every new automation is bespoke effort, and the whole thing depends on the few people who have the skill and the time to maintain it. The more successful the automations get, the more fragile the organisation quietly becomes.

## Why it compounds

A person doing a task manually delivers that value once. An automated process delivers it every time, indefinitely, without asking for more headcount. That difference doesn't show up in a quarter — it shows up as a curve, not a point. Treating automation as a capacity strategy means making a portfolio decision: which recurring effort do we convert into standing capacity, on purpose, so the value keeps accumulating after the project is "done." Treat it as a side project instead, and you never get the curve. You get isolated wins that reset every time the person who built them moves on.

## How I push for it

I don't sell automation on enthusiasm. Before anything gets built, I run the same test every time: what does this cost today, against what it costs to build. If it can't justify itself financially, it doesn't happen — no exceptions for "it'd be nice to have." That discipline is also what keeps automation from becoming a pet project instead of a strategy: if you can't make the business case, you don't have a capacity strategy, you have a hobby with a budget line.

And it isn't only about scripts and agents. The same discipline shows up in everything I do — order, structure, standardised ways of working. Automation is just the sharpest expression of it. The underlying habit is the same: don't let value depend on memory, improvisation, or one person's goodwill.

## Practicing it on myself

I don't just argue this at work. Outside it, I run a genuinely large, actively maintained system that automates the processes of being me — how I track my own career decisions, how I develop and publish what I write, how I capture what I learn. Not one tool. A network of small systems, each owning exactly one job, talking to each other through a shared structure, mostly running themselves. I step in for the judgment calls: what's true, what matters, what ships. Everything routine happens without me.

Same principle, smaller scale, no one to blame if it's wrong but me. That's the version of this belief I'd stake anything on.
